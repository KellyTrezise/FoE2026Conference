Understanding the experience of school leaders working part time
Pauline Thompson, Ryan Dunn

This study investigates the lived experience of school leaders employed part time, examining challenges, tensions and enablers to inform policy and practice. Motivated by concerns about teacher retention and constrained leadership pipelines, and by evidence that many teachers work part time yet few hold leadership roles, the research addresses three questions: what is it like to lead while part time, what tensions arise, and what enables part‑time leadership.

A qualitative design using semi‑structured interviews with fifteen part‑time school leaders captured career journeys, role expectations and barriers. Analysis was guided by Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory and employed content and constant comparative techniques; researchers conducted vertical and horizontal analyses, independently developed thematic master lists, reconciled differences by consensus and quantified themes across ecological levels.

Findings mapped to microsystem (workplace relationships and senior attitudes), mesosystem (home–work boundary negotiations and time pressures), exosystem (institutional policies expecting parity with full‑time duties), macrosystem (cultural norms, gendered caregiving expectations) and chronosystem (labour‑market changes and teacher shortages). The ecological lens elucidates how layered influences restrict or enable part‑time leadership, helps explain gendered representation in senior roles and offers timely evidence for equitable workforce planning and flexible leadership arrangements and recommendations for implementation in schools nationally.

Zero sum game? How does school leaders strategically manage resources within a resource constrained environment?
Stanley Koh, A/Prof Jenny Chesters, Dr Donnie Adams, Ms Natalie Calleja, Ms Dee Groves

School leadership matters greatly in securing better organisational and student outcomes (Leithwood et al., 2023). The main responsibility of school leaders is to provide for students’ needs, balance competing demands and keep spending within budget allocations. The ability to acquire and use resources strategically is particularly critical in jurisdictions like Victoria, where government schools frequently operate in resource constrained environments yet retain discretion over local resource use (Keddie et al., 2022).

This mixed methods study investigates how school leaders in Victorian government schools strategically manage resources to achieve school success consistent with the “growth state” or “development phase” of the school. We conducted semi‑structured interviews with school principals. Our interviews were guided by our analysis of ACARA school profile and finance data; school annual reports; school improvement plans; annual implementation plans; and other relevant documents.

This paper presents preliminary findings on the leadership practices and complex decision‑making processes that school leaders employ to prioritise competing demands, and to acquire and deploy resources strategically to maximise educational opportunities and outcomes for their school communities. Drawing on these insights, the paper offers recommendations to strengthen principals’ professional practice in strategic resource management, encompassing initial leadership training and preparation for aspiring principals, and ongoing professional development for serving principals.

The importance of school structure and culture in ways senior leaders support middle leader success
Caitlin Lamont, David Gurr, Lawrie Drysdale

It is well established that middle leaders require the support of senior leaders in order to successfully fulfil the requirements of their role and to undertake leadership responsibilities. This presentation examines case study research which revealed that both school structural elements and school culture shaped by senior leaders facilitated middle leader success.

In the case school, a regional Victorian multi‑campus K‑12 school, nine middle leaders reported that structural elements including organisational chart and role design and geographical features of the Year 9‑12 campus contributed to ways senior leaders supported the work and initiatives of middle leaders. Further, school cultural expectations consistently promoted by senior leaders allowed middle leaders to understand that they had autonomy in the way they enacted their leadership role and affirmed values‑based decision‑making at middle leadership level.

Four senior leaders at the case school shared an understanding of what continuous improvement looked like at this school and ways that middle leaders were both drivers and contributors to this. This shared understanding provided insight into ways that school culture was enacted through senior leader practice in order to promote successful middle leadership across curriculum, wellbeing, and specialist domains.

The champion paradox: Reconsidering the role of individuals in the long-term sustainment of programs and practice
Hannah Stark, Jane Page

Evidence‑informed pedagogical approaches that prioritise rich adult–child interaction, such as the Abecedarian Approach Australia (3a), are associated with benefits for young children's language, cognitive and social‑emotional development, particularly in communities experiencing social and educational inequity. Sustaining such approaches in early childhood education and care (ECEC) is challenging, and many organisations rely on individual "champions" to carry this work.

This multiple case study examined how champions influence organisational capacity to sustain 3a implementation over ten or more years across three Australian organisations. Semi‑structured interviews were conducted with 25 senior leaders, program managers, educational leaders, practitioners, policymakers and researchers, and analysed using a critical realist approach.

Findings identify a "champion paradox": the same characteristics that make champions effective in driving high‑quality practice also generate vulnerabilities that threaten its long‑term sustainment. Three interconnected mechanisms were identified: knowledge concentration, dependency creation and system capacity prevention. These dynamics have direct implications for children's access to high‑quality practice, as consistent, intentional interactional pedagogy becomes contingent on individual educators rather than guaranteed through organisational systems. Implications for ECEC policy, leadership and system design will be discussed.

Navigating Praxis Shock in Early Career Teaching
Abbey Boyer

Praxis shock can occur at any time during a teacher’s career but is most commonly documented with early career teachers, risking disillusionment, burnout and high attrition. This paper focuses on how early career teachers experience and navigate praxis shock, and on the specific elements of practical teacher work for which they feel underprepared.

The study involves six early career teachers and six mentor teachers. Early career teachers submitted video diaries documenting teaching experiences, mentoring support and how their learnings from ITE are conceptualised in practice. All participants then engaged in one‑to‑one semi‑structured interviews. The data are analysed using the Theory of Practice Architectures to examine how cultural‑discursive, material‑economic and social‑political arrangements enable and constrain early career teaching.

This analysis explores the disconnect between ITE and school settings, particularly around routine but complex aspects of teacher work such as classroom management, assessment, administrative demands and relational labour. I argue that the transition from ITE into teaching should be conceptualised not as a deficit in individual teachers, but as a structural phenomenon shaped by the practice architectures, the cultural‑discursive, material‑economic and social‑political arrangements that constitute early career teachers’ work.

Best Practices for Synchronous Online Intensive Learning (SOIL) in ITE
Emma Stevenson, Merryn Dawborn-Gundlach, Kristen Hebden, Chloe Nelson

This presentation draws on a qualitative study of a synchronous online intensive learning (SOIL) initial teacher education (ITE) program. Teacher Educators shared evidence based practices and practical recommendations for design and implementation.

Using interviews (n=9) and surveys (n=8) with teacher educators, thematic analysis identified three interrelated practices perceived as most supportive. First, diverse approaches to learning to be a teacher: multimodal activities, role play, off screen hands on tasks, including the strategic use of breakout rooms to foster choice and community. Second, working in shared digital platforms (OneNote, Miro, Google Slides, Padlet) to practise teaching, enable real time feedback, tutor monitoring and development of teaching artefacts. Third, preparing PSTs for online participation through platform orientation, leveraging pre existing rapport and logistical preparation.

The presentation will illustrate these practices with concrete examples, explaining how temporal pacing and frequent activity variation mitigate fatigue and sustain engagement while supporting activities needed for preservice teacher learning. We highlight the pedagogical and technical demands on teacher educators, outlining institutional implications, including platform selection, workload recognition and targeted professional learning. Finally, we will propose directions for research on PST outcomes and transferability across contexts and offer actionable design principles and facilitation strategies.

Cultivating Hope and Possibility Through Reciprocal Professional Learning
Pip Robinson, Ana Ward-Davies

The Professional Learning Capstone was initially designed as a response to the need for graduate teachers to become “more informed and resilient” and to develop greater sustainability as teachers in schools (Robinson, 2025, p. 182). Through a year-long, self-directed inquiry using self-study methodology (Samaras, 2011), students critically reflect on their teaching practice, developing the dispositions and practices that enable them to remain intellectually and practically engaged with their work.

This presentation considers what happens when this individually oriented inquiry becomes a collective encounter through the Capstone’s student-led conference. At the conference, students share their inquiries with peers, respond to one another’s’ work and participate in reciprocal professional learning. While the conference was conceived as an opportunity to communicate findings and receive feedback on their inquiries, students describe being excited by their peers’ work and learning new approaches to teaching with renewed enthusiasm for the profession.

We explore the conference as a pedagogical condition through which hope can emerge relationally. Rather than positioning hope as an individual disposition, we ask how the reciprocal sharing of professional inquiry enables pre-service teachers to encounter new possibilities for teaching and for themselves within the profession. What happens when a pedagogical response designed to sustain teachers becomes a space in which teachers encounter reasons to be hopeful about teaching?

Learning from machine learning: An exploratory study of teacher in‑the‑moment decision‑making and noticing incorporating computer vision as an analytical tool
Man Ching Esther Chan, Shu Jun Lee, Sachith Seneviratne, Emma Every

In this presentation, we report on the Teacher Decision Cue Project (TDCP) pilot study, a collaboration between Melbourne University and RMIT University involving a multidisciplinary team (Psychology, Education, and Computer Science). The study investigates how expert teachers make decisions during classroom teaching through an innovative combination of qualitative and computational methods.

Using multiple video cameras, including head-mounted ones worn by the instructors, we captured a single workshop co-taught by two lecturers to 13 initial teacher education students. We then conducted separate post-lesson interviews with the instructors showing them the lesson footage to understand their decision-making process during teaching.

The interviews were analysed qualitatively to identify decision cues (for example, students’ body language and facial expression) that the instructors drew upon when making decisions mid-lesson. Computer vision involving machine learning was subsequently used to model the decision cues to quantify their occurrence and salience. Importantly, the analysis distinguished between aspects of teaching and decision-making that are amenable to quantitative modelling and those that cannot be readily modelled. We reflect on our experience in this analytical process to generate discussion about the potential and limitations of computer vision in investigating teacher decision-making and noticing.

A tale of children’s ‘amader apa’ (our sister): the application of ‘reflexive rapport’ in a participatory ethnography with children living in a slum in Bangladesh

Rapport-building and reflexivity play critical roles in achieving ethical decision-making in ethnographic fieldwork. While a plethora of publications is dedicated to explaining them separately, very little is known about how ethnographers maintain reciprocity between rapport-building and reflexivity. To this end, I critically evaluate the literature, and, by utilising the stage model of rapport-building (Pitts and Miller-Day 2007) and the variants of reflexivity (Finlay 2002), I propose a conceptual and analytical framework—‘reflexive rapport’.

Drawing on my PhD fieldwork employing participatory ethnography with children living in a Bangladeshi slum, I maintain that in such fieldwork ‘reflexive rapport’ can be an effective guiding tool in building respectful and trustworthy relationships with participants, relationships in which rapport-building and reflexivity represent mutual and complementary processes. Researcher-participant relationships are entangled with contextual and circumstantial nuances associated with class, power structures, gender, and locale. In this context, I explain how, through multiple, shifting positions and negotiated interactions with the children, I became an ‘insider’—children’s “amader apa” (our sister). This chapter contributes to a growing body of literature on the conduct of ethical fieldwork, particularly with children from the margins of society.

Cultural studies goes (back) to school (and to uni and Canberra and…)
Natalie Hendry

In 1994, David Buckingham and Julian Sefton-Green published Cultural Studies Goes to School, compiling four years of research into how young people engage with popular culture and mass media in and out of schooling. Popular media have of course since shifted: from broadcast or mass media, to early internet and then social media cultures, to now an increasingly fragmented media environment of legacy media outlets, commercial platforms, and algorithmically-driven parasocial media cultures.

Even so, I return to this text (and the broader cultural studies tradition) to argue for the value of cultural studies to guide how we understand, teach and engage with digital media in education, research and policy initiatives. To do this, the presentation offers examples from two ongoing projects: Australia’s National Media Literacy Strategy (in collaboration with Alex Bacalja, Eric Fu and others), and a critical mental health and education project that extends my work exploring digital mental health cultures. I suggest that cultural studies can offer us hope and direction for theorising but also responding to the complex digital media challenges our communities encounter.

Culturally grounded pedagogies in sport and coaching education: A scoping review
Carla Luguetti, Ramon Spaaij, Karen Block, Karen Farquharson, Aish Ravi and Hayley Truskewycz

In this paper, we introduce culturally grounded pedagogies as an umbrella concept that brings together approaches variously described in the literature as culturally relevant, culturally responsive, culturally sustaining, culturally safe, and related terms. These pedagogies recognise, value, and build upon the cultural identities, knowledges, values, languages, and lived experiences of young people, positioning culture as relational, dynamic, and central to learning.

This study presents a scoping review that aims to map the existing literature on culturally grounded pedagogies in sport and coaching education, examining how these approaches have been conceptualised, theorised, and enacted, the insights of their implementation, while also identifying gaps in existing knowledge to guide future research and practice. Following the PRISMA-ScR checklist, studies were included based on the following criteria: full-text, empirical studies between 2020 and 2026, and accessed through Academic Search Complete, Education Database (ProQuest), Education Source (EBSCO), ERIC (EBSCO), Scopus, and SPORTDiscus (EBSCO). In total, 284 articles were considered for full-text review, with 21 articles moving to data extraction after having met the study’s inclusion criteria.

Findings revealed that while these pedagogies are articulated differently, they share commitments, including an explicit attention to power and inequality, an acknowledgement of cultures, racism, and colonial histories in sport, a valuing of young people’s lived experiences and relational knowledge, and a strong emphasis on reflexivity in pedagogical practice.

Oral histories with communities: Understanding education change
Sophie Rudolph, Fiza Mohd Zali, Timothy Delany, Natalie Calleja, Stephen Chatelier and Carla Luguetti

This project aimed to work with youth and education community organisations to understand how historical awareness and narratives enable opportunities for addressing present problems and imagining different futures. Drawing on “history from below” (Myers & Grosvenor, 2018), critical solidarities, and decolonial research, it adopted a participatory, community-engaged approach (Bourassa, 2021; Gawerc, 2021).

An oral history workshop brought together 13 participants from six youth and education community organisations in Melbourne. Through facilitated mapping and storytelling activities, participants reflected on organisational histories, educational and social change, and possibilities for the future. Initial findings highlighted the value of collective reflection and exchange, with participants identifying shared struggles and emerging forms of solidarity across organisations.

The workshop expanded understandings of oral history, with several organisations recognising that they were already undertaking oral history through documentaries and other community practices. Discussions also revealed how organisational histories could be lost through decisions about what was archived and remembered, highlighting the relationship between memory, power, and knowledge politics. Sharing stories made experiences of structural domination visible while creating opportunities to recognise common struggles. The workshop explored how oral history could strengthen community agency, foster solidarity, and support more equitable educational futures.

Creative, relational and reciprocal research into intergenerational arts learning
Marnee Watkins, Dr Sue Arney, Dr Jennifer Andersen

This research emerged from speculative wondering about the perceptions of our University’s undergraduates engaging with primary school children in Education Breadth arts based encounters, and implications for other areas of tertiary education. Our Faculty-funded two year (2025–26) research has drawn on a constructivist orientation with multiple forms of arts-based data generation to gain understandings of how such interactions have reframed the tertiary students’ understandings of children, self, creativity, learning, pedagogy and relationality.

This year the arts based dimension of Phase Two extended the storied narrative data from Phase One to enable participants time to voice their thinking through dialogue, performative practices, creative documentation and multimodal outputs. This allowed us to delve more deeply into their experiences and perceptions, to add nuance and subtlety to our findings, and to reveal the reciprocal benefits of the research for our teaching and identity as arts educators.

Emerging themes include: appreciation for children's creativity; new views of children reshaping adults; own changed sense of being creative and playful; affirmation for playful, embodied learning; and collaborative intergenerational play for reigniting creativity. We envisage new knowledge to the field of tertiary learning and the interactive learning young people have at University for meaningful and transformative engagement.

Artful ways of being an Educator: living, writing and teaching hopefully, ethically, radically
Jack Tan

In this paper, I present my pedagogical practice as a culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) educator working with similarly CALD students in TESOL and Creative Writing at our University. Being migrant, mobile and multicultural enable us to reflect and write creative-critically about our lived experiences as we move in our educational spaces.

These are ethical practices of valuing our multiple funds of knowledges as we negotiate our shifting educational landscape. These are also aesthetic acts of sharing and playing with our multilingual and multicultural resources, which include our languages, practices and beliefs. My method for this work is through autoethnographic poetic inquiry on pedagogical experiences. I have written about this practice as “artful ways of being an educator” (Tan, 2026), advocating for us to teach and write generously, ethically and aesthetically.

My aim in this conference paper is to share these aesthetic educational practices relevant to our work within our large University, consisting of diverse educators and students.

Improvising Hope: An experienced improvising artist creating hope, and practising to facilitate it for others.
Catherine Ryan, Prof Kylie Smith, Dr Prue Wales

My research asks: 1. How do improvisational theatre practitioners use their practice to develop and sustain relationships of care with young people?; and 2. What role can improvisation play in developing and sustaining relationships of care? Improvising and caring are relational practices, described as responsive exchanges or flows of states of being, roles, actions and affects along with ethical intentions and consequences.

Hope, generated through creative improvising practice, and shared through theatre-making facilitation with youth, is a major theme for one study participant. It’s grounded in their reflections on uncertainty, chaos, mistakes, possibilities, transformation and care. This presentation shares their reflections, and my thinking with them, alongside Gallagher and Kushnir’s (2022) Hope in a collapsing world: youth, theatre and listening as a political alternative.

Hope is debated in philosophical, psychological, religious/spiritual and political terms, considered a practice and state of being. It’s at play in varying conscious and unconscious purposes and consequences – including to sustain, motivate, care, fight and control. I try to live and work de/anti-colonially, as an uninvited guest, a white settler descendant living and working on Dja Dja Wurrung, Wurundjeri Woi-Wurrung and Bunurong Countries. So I’m following the conference’s “radical” take on acts of hope as I also think alongside Chelsea Watego’s (2021) fuck hope and Jonathan Lear’s (2008) Radical Hope: ethics in the face of cultural devastation.

Dialogic Interactions of Education Students with Australian Picture Books
Helen Cozmescu, Dr Hyejeong Ahn

This study explores how picture books can support culturally responsive, dialogic and critically conscious learning in teacher education. Situated within an Australian university context, the research investigates how education students from diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds interpret and discuss contemporary Australian picture books, and how these interactions shape understandings of culture, identity, belonging and difference.

Drawing on Freirean perspectives on dialogue, critical consciousness and the relationship between reading the word and reading the world, the study positions picture books as multimodal texts through which students can interrogate assumptions, encounter alternative perspectives and connect textual meanings with their lived experiences.

The study involves Master of Education and Master of TESOL students engaging collaboratively with selected Australian picture books, followed by facilitated dialogic discussions and reflective activities. Analysis examines how students draw on their cultural and linguistic resources, negotiate competing interpretations, question taken-for-granted assumptions and develop new understandings through dialogue.

Emerging findings suggest that picture books can function as spaces for critical encounters, enabling students to make connections between textual representations and broader social, cultural and historical contexts. The study demonstrates that picture books, when accompanied by intentional Freirean-inspired pedagogy, can move beyond developing cultural awareness towards fostering critical, dialogic and transformative approaches to teacher education.

Resilience, creativity and the ukulele: Exploring a melody-first approach to learning the ukulele for musician-teachers living with chronic illness
Fiona M. King

Ukulele learners often start with chords and singing, yet this familiar pathway may not suit all players. This study examines the experiences of musician-teachers living with chronic illness as they engage with the C Major Method, a melody-first ‘fingerstyle’ approach for ukulele playing that foregrounds improvised melody for instrumental learning, sustained musicianship and creative expression.

The research design is a qualitative collective intrinsic case study that involves three cases: interviews with another learner of the method (Case 1), the researcher’s longitudinal practitioner inquiry reflections and compositions (Case 2), and a three-lesson teaching sequence pilot with an adult musician-teacher (Case 3). Data are analysed thematically across the cases.

In times of polycrisis, educators are increasingly asked to be resilient: to adapt, persist and continue teaching amid intensifying pressures of burnout, standardisation, precarity and social uncertainty. What might become possible if resilience were understood not as an individual capacity to withstand crisis, but as a relational and reciprocal practice of sustaining one another?

This paper draws on my Doctor of Education inquiry into how critical and creative practices enable teachers and students to meaningfully work through polycrisis cultivating hopeful conditions in which other futures can become imaginable. The research unsettles conventional distinctions between research process, finding and output.

Collaborative making throughout the research led to the development of a professional learning zine that invites teachers to articulate experience and, through collaborative sharing and making, imagine possibilities beyond individualised resilience. Importantly, this zine is not just research output, but a relational infrastructure that emphasises hope, reciprocity and professional mattering.

I propose that hope is radical when it becomes a practice of making otherwise: making relationships, making meaning, making research and making possible futures together. In this sense, hope is neither optimism nor an individual disposition, but a relational infrastructure cultivated through collaboration, materials and pedagogies.

Making Learning Visible: Exploring Pre-service Teachers' Academic Literacy Practices through Arts-Rich Translanguaging Pedagogy
Melissa Jufenna Slamet, Julie Choi, Yvette Slaughter

In increasingly diverse educational contexts, pre-service teachers need opportunities to engage with academic learning in ways that recognise the linguistic, cultural, and semiotic resources they bring to teacher education. Arts-Rich Translanguaging Pedagogy (ARTP) offers one such possibility by creating spaces for multilingual, multimodal, and arts-based meaning-making.

This paper explores how ARTP supported pre-service teachers' academic literacy practices in a Master of TESOL course at the University of Melbourne.

Drawing on language assemblages theory, the study examines participants' engagement in arts-rich learning activities, including identity text creation, multimodal composing, and dialogic reflections. Data comprise participants' artefacts, written reflections, classroom interactions, and interviews.

Findings suggest that ARTP facilitated meaningful engagement by enabling pre-service teachers to connect personal histories with academic concepts, mobilise diverse resources for learning, and critically examine assumptions about language, literacy, and teaching.

Through an ARTP lens, literacy practices extend beyond cognitive processes to encompass affective, ideological, performative, and aspirational dimensions of meaning-making. These often-overlooked dimensions illuminate how pre-service teachers develop digital literacies, sustain motivation in learning, and cultivate criticality for navigating linguistically and culturally diverse contexts. The study highlights institutional conditions and contributes to a pedagogical framework of three meaning-making orientations that support meaningful learning in teacher education.

Assessing the Value of Dynamic Assessment of Early Numeracy Abilities across the Preschool-to-School Transition
Nathalie Parry, Kelly Trezise, Robert Reeve, Sean Kang, Lorraine Graham, Bert De Smedt

Characterising preschoolers’ early numeracy is crucial, as these skills strongly predict later mathematics achievement, and inform timely, targeted support. Standardised assessments capture performance at a single time point, yet are limited in elucidating how numeracy skills emerge and develop.

Dynamic assessment may offer a more nuanced picture by systematically offering hints when a child is unable to solve a problem independently, thereby capturing emerging skills.

This study examined the efficacy of a dynamic assessment approach for characterising preschool children’s early numeracy abilities and its capacity to predict numeracy performance in the early years of schooling. We followed 44 children over two years from preschool through formal schooling, assessing early number skills in three domains (number, relations, and operations).

In preschool, children completed dynamic assessment of eight numeracy skills and the Test of Early Mathematics Abilities (TEMA-3), which was re-administered Grade 1.

We examined children’s responsiveness to support within the dynamic assessment using mixed-effects logistic regressions; support significantly improved accuracy, although gains varied according to numeracy skill, item difficulty, and number of prompts.

Linear regression analyses indicated that Grade 1 TEMA-3 scores were better predicted by preschool dynamic assessment (z = 2.84, p<.01) than by preschool TEMA-3 (z=1.56, p>.05) overall and across domains. These findings provide insight into skill-specific differences in responsiveness to support and suggest that prompt requirements may indicate individual differences in early numeracy performance.

The study highlights the efficacy of dynamic over conventional assessments for predicting numeracy abilities in school, with implications for research and educational instruction.

Active assessing: Supporting academic achievement and well-being at the teaching-learning-assessing coalface
John Quay

Assessment is commonly described using various appended adjectives – summative assessment, formative assessment, authentic assessment, programme-based assessment – and there are many more. In this session I introduce the idea of “active assessing” and: (1) how this is applicable in a unit of work (or a subject in university terms); (2) how this supports academic achievement; (3) how this supports well-being.

And finally I turn to questions that ideas around active assessing may raise for university teaching-learning-assessing.

Educators on Screen: A Global Database for Educational Research
Hugh Gundlach

Global teacher shortages make it essential to understand and raise the cultural status of teaching. Evidence shows that teachers’ attraction to—and retention in—the profession closely tracks societal status and social approval: how teachers are regarded by peers, families and the wider public is a stronger predictor of career longevity than many other factors.

Recent analyses of policy and news coverage reveal a growing negative tone in portrayals of teachers. Popular culture too, especially film and television, plays a powerful role in shaping those perceptions.

This presentation launches Educators on Screen, believed to be the world’s largest searchable database of fictional teachers in film and television. It documents over 2,000 teachers from more than 1,000 films and TV series across 125+ years, with demographic data on teachers and student populations from early learning to higher education.

See the database in action and learn how dominant narrative tropes shape public attitudes and the consequences for recruitment, retention and professional respect. The resource, featured on ABC TV and radio, offers unique datasets for researchers across diverse disciplines within the Faculty.
